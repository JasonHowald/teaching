21. Introduction to Statistical Hypotheses

Using statistics to support a statement of fact.

Hypothesis testing concepts

1 Preparatory work

Read: OpenIntro Statistics Section 6.1 and 7.1

2 What is hypothesis testing?

A hypothesis is an idea or theory which can be tested.

Statistical evidence is evidence based on data. It’s usually subject to measurement error and sampling error.

A hypothesis test is a fair, objective procedure for weighing the strength of statistical evidence.

These procedures protect the integrity of science. They can’t remediate the effects of bad faith actors such as liars and tyrants.

3 Why isn’t simple observation enough?

  1. I’m concerned whether a coin is fair. I flipped it 100 times to check for bias. It landed heads 60 times. How do you interpret this evidence?

  2. Does this drug lower blood pressure? In 30 patients on average bp went down 10 units. How strong is this evidence?

4 Anatomy of a hypothesis test

  1. Formulate hypothesis. Make it a testable, numerical statement of difference.
  2. Collect data. Avoid bias.
  3. Is the data consistent with hypothesis?
    1. If no, announce no evidence.
    2. If yes, is the measured effect plausibly due to chance?
      1. if yes, announce inconclusive result.
      2. if no, announce statistically significant result

3b is the tricky bit. It requires the probability, if the hypothesis were wrong, of finding the effect from chance measurement error.

5 Hypothesis test, simple example

In the 2012 World Series, the San Francisco Giants swept the Detroit Tigers (4-0) by winning four games in a row. Which statements best support the claim that the Giants were a better team?

  • Hypothesis: In any given game, \(P(\text{ Giants win }) > .5\)
  • The 4-0 sweep is the data.
  • Is the data consistent with hypothesis? Yes
  • Is the effect plausibly due to chance? If they were only equally good, \(P(\text{ four wins }) = 1/16 \approx 6\%\). Yes, the result is plausibly due to chance.

Verdict: Inconclusive

(Note: The number \(1/16\) is the p-value of the test. See below.)

6 Hypothesis test, second example

You suspect a die is rigged not to roll “1”.

  • Hypothesis: \(P(\text{ landing 1 }) < 1/6\)
  • The evidence: Out of 30 rolls, the die never lands “1”.
  • Is the data consistent with hypothesis? Yes
  • Is the effect plausibly due to chance? The chance of no 1’s out of 30 rolls on a fair die is \((5/6)^{30} = 0.00421\). No, the result is not plausibly due to chance.

Verdict: Statistically Significant Evidence. The die is unfair.

(Note: The number \(.00421\) is the p-value of the test. See below.)

7 Changing an idea into a hypothesis

Idea Hypothesis
“Global temperatures are rising.” \(\mu_{2026} > \mu_{2006}\)
“These managers will show bias against women in promotion decisions.” \(p_m > p_w\)
“Dogs are smarter than cats.” \(\mu_d > \mu_c\)
“This machine produces parts that are larger than the specification.” \(\mu > 0.75\)
“Ammonium nitrogen fertilizer will improve my tomato yield.” \(\mu_1 < \mu_2\)
“This coin is biased.” \(p \neq 0.5\)

8 Notes on hypothesis statements

A good hypothesis statement should:

  • Assert a difference or inequality.
  • Refer to parameters, not statistics.

9 Typical hypotheses

  1. Compare mean to baseline: \(\mu >10\)
  2. Compare two means \(\mu_1 > \mu_2\).
  3. Compare proportion to baseline: \(p > 0.5\)
  4. Compare two proportions: \(p_m > p_w\).

10 Two-sided hypotheses

A two-sided hypothesis states a difference but doesn’t say which way. Both high and low evidence can support a two-sided hypothesis.

  • \(\mu \neq 10\)
  • \(\mu_1 \neq \mu_2\).
  • \(p \neq 0.5\)
  • \(p_m \neq p_w\).

11 The Counterpoint – Null Hypothesis

Hypothesis \(H_A\) Null Hypothesis \(H_0\)
Global temps are rising Global temps are constant
Managers biased vs. women Managers are fair
\(\mu > 0.75\) \(\mu = 0.75\)
\(p_A > p_B\) \(p_A = p_B\)

The job of the Null Hypothesis is to assert no difference, not to reverse the difference.

When we ask “Is the effect plausibly due to chance?” we mean in a world where the Null Hypothesis were true.

12 P-value and threshold

A hypothesis test hinges on the plausibility that our results are due to chance.

We need the probability, in a Null Hypothesis world, of getting results as strong as those witnessed. This probability is called the p-value. If the p-value is large, the results are plausibly due to chance. If the p-value is small, the results are not due to chance and so are statistically significant.

The cut-off between “large” and “small” p-values is often chosen to be \(0.05\), and this choice is called the threshold of significance.
