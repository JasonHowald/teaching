20. Standard Error and Confidence Intervals

All measurement has potential error. With care we can compensate.

Preparatory work

Read: OpenIntro Statistics Section 5.1 and 5.2

Correct each statement – statistics vs. parameters.

  1. I sampled 500 patients and averaged their blood pressure. Now I know the population average blood pressure.
  2. I surveyed 500 teens and calculated a bullying risk probability. Now I know a parameter.
  3. I sampled 500 library books. Now I know the average length of all books.
  4. I weighed 500 cats. Now I know μ, the average weight of cats.

The problem

  • Statistical measurements only estimate parameters. When a statistic is used to estimate a parameter, we call it a point estimate.
  • Statistical measurement error is natural and inevitable. Point estimates naturally differ from the parameters they intend to measure.

The compensation

  • Large samples allow us to limit measurement error.
  • Special formulas (for SE) allow us to quantify and predict measurement error.
  • Confidence intervals allow us to responsibly report our point estimates and their potential error in a responsible way.

The good news

  • Usually, measurement error is not just random, but normal N(0,…) with mean 0 and predictable deviation.
  • The deviation of the error of a point estimate is called the Standard Error (SE).

Visualizing Standard Error

Adjust the sliders to see how the sample size (\(n\)) and the number of resamples (\(m\)) affect the distribution of the mean.

  1. Set \(n=25\). When \(m=1\), what happens? Why is the single red dot close to \(10\)? Why isn’t it exactly at \(10\)? Why isn’t it at the same place as on your neighbor’s screen?

  2. Raise \(m\). Why are the red dots close to \(10\)? Why do they vary? How much do they stray from \(10\), typically? That’s Standard Error

  3. Keeping \(m\) somewhat large, Raise \(n\). Do the red dots spread out farther from \(10\) or cluster more closely? Has standard error gone up or down?

  4. With large \(m\), set \(n=1\). Why does the right-hand plot look like that?

  5. The original population is quite skewed. For large \(n\), is the sampling distribution skewed or normal?

Three huge takeaways.

  1. The Standard Error is the standard deviation of the sampling distribution. It depends on the deviation in the original population and also depends on \(n\).

  2. Large-sample (i.e. big n) averages cluster tightly around the true mean. This is the Law of Large Numbers.

  3. Regardless of the shape of the original distribution, large-sample averages follow a normal bell curve. This is the Central Limit Theorem. The bell curve’s mean and variability are described in parts 1 & 2 above.

Good news: There are formulas

We don’t have to calculate standard errors by simulating sampling distributions. There are formulas.

Parameter Point Estimate SE Formula Variable Type
Mean \(\mu\) \(\bar{x}\) \(\displaystyle \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\) Quantitative
Proportion \(p\) \(\hat{p}\) \(\displaystyle \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}\) Categorical
Difference \(\text{diff}\) \(\displaystyle \sqrt{SE_1^2 + SE_2^2}\) Comparison

SE as the basis for Confidence Intervals

You have a point estimate for a parameter. It approximates a parameter. You want to guess the parameter.

The Empirical Rule says that 95% of the time, the measurement error is less than \(2*SE\), so the confidence interval: \[\text{ point estimate } \pm 2*SE\] almost certainly captures the parameter.
