Calculating probabilities by counting sample spaces

2 The Language of Probability

Any process that leads to an unpredictable result is a random process. It can be…

  • “Truly” randomized: coin flip, card draw, die roll, quantum measurement, etc.
  • Complex and unpredictable: Weather, voting, disease spread, manufacturing error, a two-year-old’s mood, etc.

A random variable is any variable (numeric or categorical) which depends on a random process.

  1. Result of a coin flip (“H”) or die roll (“3”)
  2. The day’s temperature
  3. Number of students who enroll in a class.
  4. The age, height, or religion of a person (randomly selected from a population).

3 The Language of Probability

The Sample Space is the set of outcomes of a random process.

  • All possible results of four coin flips.
  • All possible orderings of a standard deck of 52 cards.
  • All possible rolls of two dice.
  • All possible weather patterns today.

An Event is a single outcome or a collection of outcomes.

  • All four coins land heads (single outcome), or more heads than tails (collection of outcomes)
  • Cards in sorted order, or top card is 2❤️
  • Two dice total 12 (one outcome), or total 7 (collection)
  • Rainy (collection of outcomes)

4 The Language of Probability

For any event \(A\), \(P(A)\) mean its probability.

If we flip a coins, \(P(\) It lands heads \() = 1/2\)

If we roll two dice, \(P(\) Each lands 6 \() = 1/36\)

Often…

\(P(A) = \frac{ \text{Number of successful outcomes} }{\text{Number of outcomes}}\)

5 What probability is implied?

A probability is always a number between 0 and 1. For each expression, what probability is meant?

“Could go either way. It’s 50/50.”

“It’s a 98% chance.”

“The disease has a 0.2% mortality rate.“

“It’s one in twenty.”

“It’s impossible. Not gonna happen.”

[Bonus] “I give it four-to-one odds.“

6 Probabilities by counting

\(P(A) = \frac{ \text{Number of successful outcomes} }{\text{Number of outcomes}}\)

I rolled three dice and they totaled 5. What are the chances!

  1. Identify the random process: Roll three dice
  2. Count all possible outcomes, using multiplication.
  3. Count all outcomes which lead to a total of \(5\). Hint: Just list them. 1+2+2 is different from 2+2+1.
  4. Divide for the probability.

7 Equally probable outcomes

\(P(A) = \frac{ \text{Number of successful outcomes} }{\text{Number of outcomes}}\) is only sometimes warranted

Formula works for:

  1. The two results of a coin flip.
  2. The six results of a die roll.
  3. The 36 results of two die rolls.
  4. The 52 results of a card draw from a deck.

Formula fails for:

  1. The 11 resulting totals (2 through 12) from rolling two dice.
  2. The two possible results (positive/negative) from some bloodwork.
  3. The two possible winners of a game.
  4. The six people she might ask for help.

8 Winning a contest.

The problem: Two sports teams (A and B) are about evenly matched: Any game between them is a 50/50 toss up. They play five games. What is the probability that team A wins at least four?

Step 1: What are the “outcomes”?

Step 2: How many outcomes are possible? Multiplication is your friend!

Step 3: How many outcomes are successes?

Divide and conclude.

Bonus: You watch a contest between two teams, and one team wins four games out of five. How confident should you be that the winning team is actually better?

9 A Flawed Classic

The problem: A Dad of exactly two kids is asked whether he has a daughter. “Yes” he replies, but does not elaborate. You take this to mean he has at least one daughter. What is the probability that he also has a son?

Option 1: The other kid

The other kid could be male or female. P(son) = 1/2.

Option 2: Family structures

Four equally likely kid-gender outcomes are possible: MM, MF, FM, FF. Eliminate the first. Of the remaining 3, two are “successes.” P(son) = 2/3.

Which argument is more correct?

This classic puzzle depends on several oversimplifications about the world. Identify them.

10 The Monty Hall Problem

This famous math problem is named after Monty Hall, the host of an old game show called “Let’s Make a Deal.”

You’re in a game show in which you have to make a choice between three doors. One door hides a prize, and two doors hide nothing of value. Whenever this game is played, the host waits for the contestant to choose, then opens and reveals a door which has no prize. Afterwards, the contestant may change their choice before the final reveal. Should you switch to the other door before the big reveal?

It doesn’t matter. A door has been wasted, leaving a 50/50 chance of success with either remaining door.

You should switch. The initial choice has 1/3 chance, and the remaining door a has 2/3 chance.

12 A chance at Magic

A (Magic The Gathering commander-format) deck has 99 cards, of which 36 are “land,” making them immediately useful at the game’s beginning. You draw an initial hand of seven cards.

  1. What is the probability of getting no land cards among them?
  2. How many draws of seven are possible from a deck of 99 cards?
  3. How many draws of seven have no land cards?
  4. Divide to find the probability.
